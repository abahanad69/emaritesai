What is a computer-use agent, and why is Apple worried?

These AI tools see your screen, click and type for you, and that power needs careful limits.

2026-10-03 · EmaritesAI

A regular chatbot answers in text. A computer-use agent acts. It takes a screenshot, works out what is on screen, picks the next step, then moves the mouse, clicks or types. It repeats that loop until the task is done or it gets stuck.

This lets AI handle work that has no clean API: filling web forms, moving data between apps or tidying files. Several AI labs now offer this ability to developers, and desktop assistants increasingly ship it to everyday users.

The catch is access. To be useful, an agent may need your browser, files, email or messages. Whatever it can reach, a bug, a malicious web page or a misunderstood instruction can also reach. That is why Apple said this week that it will tighten Full Disk Access on Macs, warning that the risks of broad access grow as agents become more autonomous.

Agents also make mistakes. They can misread a button or act on hidden instructions planted in a page, a problem known as prompt injection. Good tools ask for confirmation before sensitive steps such as payments, sending messages or deleting files.

The sensible approach is to treat an agent like a new assistant: give it only the access the task needs, watch the first few runs and keep important actions behind your own approval.

Explained simply
  1. Look: The agent takes a screenshot and works out what is on screen.
  2. Decide: The model picks the next action needed to reach your goal.
  3. Act: It moves the mouse, clicks or types, just as a person would.
  4. Repeat: It checks the new screen and loops until the task is done.
  5. Stay safe: Limit its access and confirm payments, sends and deletes yourself.
3 things to know
  1. Computer-use agents see the screen, then click and type to finish tasks
  2. Every permission you grant widens what can go wrong
  3. Grant minimum access and approve sensitive steps yourself
