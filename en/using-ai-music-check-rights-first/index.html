Using AI music in a video? Check these 4 rights first

Who owns an AI track depends on the tool's plan, how much you added yourself and where you publish it.

2026-10-15 · EmaritesAI

AI music tools can produce a full song in seconds, and streaming platforms are now sorting those songs into labelled categories. Before you use one in a business video, an ad or a release, a few checks can save you a takedown or a dispute.

Start with the tool's own terms, because ownership often depends on the plan you were on when you made the track. Suno's help centre says songs made on its free Basic plan belong to Suno and can be used only for non-commercial purposes, while subscribers to its Pro or Premier plans own the songs they create and keep commercial rights after cancelling.

Next, think about copyright. In its January 2025 report, the US Copyright Office said that prompts alone do not make you the author of the output. Your own lyrics, melody, edits or arrangement can be protected, but the purely generated parts may not be, so others could reuse them.

Then disclose. YouTube asks music partners to mark tracks as Fully Gen AI, Partly Gen AI or No Gen AI, and it may label songs itself if you do not. Rules differ outside the US, so check local law before a commercial release.

Step by step
  1. Read the plan terms: Check who owns tracks made on your plan and whether commercial use is allowed.
  2. Check the plan at creation: Suno ties ownership to the plan you had when you made the song.
  3. Add your own work: Write lyrics, a melody or edits so part of the track is clearly yours.
  4. Keep records: Save prompts, dates, versions and your plan receipt for any later dispute.
  5. Disclose on upload: Mark AI use honestly, such as YouTube's Fully or Partly Gen AI options.
3 things to know
  1. Ownership often depends on the plan you used when you made the track
  2. US Copyright Office: prompts alone do not make you the author
  3. Platforms like YouTube want AI use disclosed and may label it
