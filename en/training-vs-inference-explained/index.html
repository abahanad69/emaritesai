Training vs inference: where does AI's computing power go?

Every AI model lives two lives: one learning, one answering you.

2026-10-02 · EmaritesAI

Every AI model goes through two very different phases. Training comes first: the model studies enormous amounts of text, images or code and slowly adjusts billions of internal settings until its predictions improve. It is a one-off project that can run for weeks across thousands of GPUs.

Inference is everything after that. Each time you send a prompt, the finished model runs once to produce an answer. A single answer is cheap, but popular models answer millions of questions a day, so inference becomes a large, never-ending bill.

That is why labs now compete on inference as well as on intelligence. Faster chips, smarter software and smaller models all cut the time and cost of each reply. OpenAI's new Ultrafast tier for GPT-6 Astra, served on NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs, is one example: same model, faster output, higher price.

For users, the split explains a lot. A model's knowledge is fixed at training time, which is why it can miss recent events. Its speed and price, however, depend on how it is served, which is why the same model can come in fast and slow versions.

Explained simply
  1. Training: The model learns patterns from massive datasets over weeks of GPU time.
  2. Weights: What it learned is stored as billions of numbers that stay fixed after training.
  3. Inference: Each prompt runs the finished model once to generate a reply, token by token.
  4. Serving: Chips and software decide how fast and how cheaply those replies arrive.
3 things to know
  1. Training teaches the model once; inference runs every time you ask
  2. Inference costs grow with every user, so labs race to make it faster
  3. Knowledge is fixed at training; speed and price depend on serving
Sources: NVIDIA Blog - Training vs inference, NVIDIA Blog - GPT-6 Astra Ultrafast
✅ Ultrafast is a faster serving tier of the same GPT-6 Astra model on Blackwell GPUs
✅ Training happens once; inference runs per request
