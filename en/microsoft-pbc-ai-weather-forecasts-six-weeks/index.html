Microsoft's open AI tool sharpens weather forecasts up to 6 weeks out

Probabilistic Bias Correction fixes systematic errors in existing forecast models and runs on ordinary CPUs.

2026-10-15 · EmaritesAI

Weather forecasts become much less reliable beyond about two weeks, whether they come from physics simulations or AI models. Researchers at Microsoft, MIT, the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) and other institutions say they have a cheaper way to improve the two-to-six-week range, and published the work in Nature this week. The software is open source.

The method, called Probabilistic Bias Correction (PBC), does not replace existing forecast models. It uses machine learning to find the systematic errors they make and correct them. One example from Microsoft researcher Lester Mackey is climate change: a model trained on historical records can lean toward temperatures colder than today's.

Applied to ECMWF's main physics-based model, PBC improved skill for 98% of precipitation targets, 92% of temperature targets and 91% of pressure targets, according to the paper. It also doubled the modest subseasonal skill of ECMWF's AI Forecasting System. In ECMWF's 2025 real-time AI Weather Quest competition, PBC's global forecasts ranked first for every variable and lead time.

The tool runs on CPUs rather than supercomputers or large GPU clusters, Mackey told The Verge, which could put it within reach of weather agencies with small budgets. Co-author Genevieve Flaspohler now leads the nonprofit Rhiza Research, which is testing PBC with Kenya's national weather service, with Gates Foundation funding, ahead of an expected extreme rainy season. ECMWF's Matthew Chantry said turning such research into operational forecasting is the hardest step.

3 things to know
  1. PBC corrects bias in existing models for forecasts 2 to 6 weeks ahead
  2. It improved ECMWF's model on 98% of rain and 92% of temperature targets
  3. The open-source tool runs on CPUs, not supercomputers
Sources: Paper: Enhancing AI and Dynamical Subseasonal Forecasts with Probabilistic Bias Correction, The Verge
