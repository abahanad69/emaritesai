Why does AI run on GPUs and not ordinary processors?

AI is mostly huge batches of simple multiplications, and GPUs are built to do thousands of them at once.

2026-10-15 · EmaritesAI

Every answer a chatbot gives comes from passing numbers through a neural network, and most of that work is matrix math: multiplying long lists of numbers and adding the results, billions of times over. The question is which chip does that fastest.

A CPU, the main processor in a laptop, has a few powerful cores with large caches. It is designed for low latency, handling a handful of tasks quickly, one after another. A GPU, first built to draw graphics, takes the opposite approach. NVIDIA describes it as hundreds of cores able to run thousands of threads at the same time, splitting a big problem into many small pieces and solving them together.

Matrix math splits that way naturally, since each multiplication in a layer can happen independently. Modern AI GPUs add special units, which NVIDIA calls Tensor Cores, that perform a multiply and an add in a single step. The result is more work done per unit of energy than on a CPU.

This is why chip supply drives the AI business. Training a frontier model and serving it to millions of users both need large numbers of GPUs, which is why the share prices of chipmakers move with every sign of how much AI companies plan to spend.

Explained simply
  1. Count the math: A model answers by multiplying and adding numbers billions of times.
  2. Meet the CPU: A few strong cores handle tasks one after another, with low delay.
  3. Meet the GPU: Hundreds of cores run thousands of threads in parallel.
  4. Add Tensor Cores: Special units do a multiply and an add in one step.
  5. See the stakes: More AI users means more GPUs, so chip supply shapes the industry.
3 things to know
  1. AI work is mostly matrix math that splits into many parallel pieces
  2. CPUs have a few strong cores; GPUs run thousands of threads at once
  3. That is why GPU supply and AI spending move chip stocks
