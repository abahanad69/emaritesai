Margaret Hamilton, who led Apollo's flight software, dies at 90

MIT says the computer scientist, who helped turn software engineering into a discipline, died on 30 September.

2026-10-08 · EmaritesAI

Margaret Hamilton, the computer scientist who led the software engineering team at MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory during NASA's Apollo programme, died on 30 September at the age of 90, MIT announced on 7 October.

Born in Paoli, Indiana, in 1936, Hamilton started programming at MIT in 1959 on weather prediction software with meteorologist Edward Lorenz, then worked on SAGE, the first US air defence system, at MIT Lincoln Laboratory. In 1965 she joined the Instrumentation Lab, which had the NASA contract for Apollo's onboard flight software, as the project's first programmer at MIT and its first woman programmer. By 1968 she was assistant director of the command and service module team, and more than 400 people were working on Apollo software.

Her team's software ranked tasks by priority. During the Apollo 11 landing in 1969, a 1202 alarm warned that the computer was overloaded, and the system dropped less important jobs to protect the ones the landing needed, so the descent continued.

Hamilton used the term "software engineering" to argue that writing software deserved the same rigour as other engineering. She later founded Higher Order Software in 1976 and then Hamilton Technologies, wrote more than 130 publications, and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom from President Barack Obama in 2016.

Her focus on catching errors before they cause harm is now a central question for software written with the help of AI.
