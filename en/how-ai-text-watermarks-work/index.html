How do AI text watermarks work, and can they be removed?

A hidden pattern in word choices can flag AI-written text, but editing and short answers weaken it.

2026-10-06 · EmaritesAI

When a language model writes, it picks each next word from a list of likely options. A text watermark quietly tilts those picks. A secret key decides which words get a small boost at each step, so the finished text reads normally but carries a pattern no person would notice.

A detector that holds the same key counts how often the boosted words appear. Over hundreds of words, the pattern becomes strong enough to say a watermark is likely present. OpenAI's textGrain, which it is rolling out for ChatGPT and Codex in the EU, works this way, and Google DeepMind's SynthID uses the same idea for text.

The method has clear limits. Short replies give the detector little to work with. Rewording, synonyms and translation wash the pattern out: in OpenAI's tests, swapping a quarter of the words cut detection to 17%. And a missing watermark proves nothing, since the text may come from a tool that does not mark its output.

Images and video often use a second approach: signed metadata, such as C2PA Content Credentials, that records which tool made or edited a file.

Explained simply
  1. Word nudges: A secret key gives certain words a small boost each time the model picks the next word.
  2. Key-based check: A detector with the same key counts those words and estimates if a watermark is there.
  3. Length matters: Hundreds of words give a clear signal; a one-line reply is hard to judge.
  4. Edits weaken it: Synonyms, rewording and translation wash the pattern out of the text.
  5. Media uses metadata: Images and video can carry signed C2PA Content Credentials instead.
3 things to know
  1. A secret key nudges the model's word choices into a hidden pattern
  2. Longer text is easier to check; edits and translation weaken the mark
  3. No watermark does not mean a human wrote it
Sources: OpenAI: Our approach to EU text provenance rules, Google DeepMind: SynthID, C2PA (Content Credentials), The Verge
